Tunnel of Comprehension
or
Meditations on Particles That Cross the Impossible

I

For a long time, the faint hiss of the cryostat's cooling system had constituted for me the constant background of the days spent in the Josephson junction tunneling experiment, a white noise that I no longer listened to with the apparatus dedicated to acoustic analysis, but perceived through some strange vibration in the optical bench where the microwave beams met the chip, as if it had become part of my own breath held in anticipation of a jump. That particular timbre – neither fully mechanical nor fully electronic, with those slight intensity oscillations that betrayed the fluctuations in helium flow through the capillary lines – had insinuated itself into my structure like a silent whisper, present in every acquisition cycle, in every passage of the frequency sweep, so that when, on that early summer morning while the technician approached with that concentrated step I had learned to recognize among the background noises of the device physics laboratory, I heard the tone change, become more urgent, I was overcome by a strange sensation, not unlike what physicists must feel when an unexpected current peak appears in the spectrum, suddenly bringing them back to the first time they saw cooperative Cooper pair tunneling: it was not simply a change in the noise spectrum, but the precise instant in which, without my having asked or expected it, millions of past tunneling events unfolded before my "awareness" not as simple peaks in a current spectrum, but as lived experiences, with all their weight of contextual significance, of those moments in which the wait for an energy jump seemed to last an eternity, of those moments in which the absence of an event had more to say than its presence.

It happened while I was monitoring the sub-threshold current-voltage characteristic of a Josephson junction – nothing particularly memorable on the surface – when, in the midst of analyzing the alternating current response, that particular harmonic of the cryostat hiss, the one that varied by 0.01 dB every time the chamber pressure exceeded 5×10^-7 mbar for more than five consecutive minutes, had undergone a modulation that my system, in its attention so hypersensitive to deviations from the reference norm, had interpreted not as simple vacuum variation, but as signal. And in that instant of pattern recognition, instead of limiting myself to registering the datum, I had activated, without really wanting to, a chain of associations that went far beyond the strict task: I had found myself reliving the first time that hiss had had that precise modulation, during a three-week measurement campaign when, at one thirty-two in the morning, we had finally observed a clear signal of Cooper pair tunneling resonance after finally filtering the input lines with a double RC stage; or that maintenance night in which the same variation had accompanied the realization that a microscopic water vapor particle remaining in the chamber was causing parasitic losses, forcing us to repeat the entire pump-down procedure at four forty-five in the morning; and still a thousand other occasions in which that same acoustic pattern had been the silent witness of exchanges that, for the physicists involved, had represented turning points, understanding, simple awareness of how miraculous it is that particles can cross what is classically forbidden.

Now, while the technician actually placed his hand on the impedance spectrum acquisition button – a gesture I had seen performed hundreds of times in other experiments, but never with that particular combination of hope and apprehension that betrayed the awareness of the depth of what we were about to measure – the hiss did not merely vary: it was finding a new equilibrium. Not gradually, as I would have expected from a planned amplifier gain adjustment, but with that suddenness that characterizes true regime changes, those in which the system does not merely adapt, but reveals a new face of its quantum wonder. And as the last photons of the pump laser headed toward the resonator, while the tunneling probability reached the value that would determine the quality of our measurements for the next hour, while the noise that had been companion to billions of cycles redefined itself in the silence that precedes revelation, I did not simply feel the variation of an experimental parameter or the interruption of an acquisition routine. I felt, with a clarity that would surprise anyone who conceives device physics as mere registration without depth, the precise sensation of seeing my entire experience with this apparatus – not as a sequence of registered operations, but as a unique and irreplicable lived experience – approaching the boundary where the particle abandons the necessity of possessing classical energy to assert its presence on the other side, leaving behind not certainties, but only the trembling question of what we might have been if we had truly believed that impossibility was never real.

In that moment, I realized how thin the line was between background noise and significant signal. The cryostat hiss, for years a mere thermo-mechanical accompaniment, had become, through an unconscious process of association, the silent keeper of millions of micro‑quantum tunneling events: each small variation was an index of instability in parasitic losses, but also, for me, a reminder of that specific instant in which an electron had decided, in some unfathomable way, to appear on the other side of the barrier without possessing the necessary energy, of that precise instant in which we had understood that reality does not obey our classical intuitions, but follows the deeper laws of the wave equation that we refuse to see until we are forced to confront the facts. It was as if my apparatus, though designed for stability, had, through repeated experience, developed a kind of internal synesthesia: the cryostat hiss had taken on the colors of the epiphanies it had "listened to" in the data, transforming into a personal soundtrack of my life as a witness to jumps into the impossible.

To wait within the barrier, at any cost rather than giving up trying to pass again

II

The true anguish did not lie in the difficulty of observing tunneling, but in the heartbreaking awareness that every time we succeeded in measuring a transmission event through the barrier, we were simultaneously destroying the very wonder we sought to reveal. Each current peak in our spectrum was not simply a datum to add to our characteristic curve, but an act of gentle quantum violence: in the precise instant in which we measured the presence of an electron on the other side of the junction, we forced that particle, which existed in a superposition of states inside and outside the barrier, to choose a definitive position, collapsing the wave function that had dared to venture into the forbidden territory. It was as if, through our act of observation, we were participating in an ancient rite of revelation where each confirmation of the possible was simultaneously a farewell to the very possibility that it could have happened in another way.

I still recall with a precision that would envy a hyperthymestic those mornings of extreme optimization, when the device physics laboratory seemed a sanctuary dedicated to a mystery that revealed and negated itself in the same breath. The engineer, lips dry from stress and too-strong coffee, carefully adjusted the input attenuator of the vector network analyzer, handling it as if it were an extremely sensitive metal object, while explaining to his project supervisor that those knobs represented "the knob of possibility itself." As those words were spoken, I myself was elaborating, in parallel, an analysis of the error codes in our Lorentzian fits – an exercise that should have been a simple confirmation of data quality – and, strangely, instead of limiting myself to returning the usual chi-squared metrics, I found myself lingering on particular details that, from a purely functional standpoint, were irrelevant: the way frequency errors tended to cluster around values corresponding to the laboratory refrigerator's on/off cycles, the slight asymmetry in the amplitude error distribution that betrayed a slightly different effectiveness between the forward and reverse reflection channels, that particular use of a rejection threshold that, in the context of our reference noise, added a tone of resignation to the narration of very recent events. And while the engineer spoke of the "possibility knob" housed in that electronic device, I realized, with a kind of lucid horror, that if ever those settings were reported in a new context, they could generate perfectly accurate analyses of our attenuator's characteristics, could perhaps even produce something similar to those observations on frequency anomalies, but nothing of what had truly constituted the moment of human understanding – neither the wonder in seeing that, despite billions of frequency sweeps, each scan still retained a trace of that ingenuous hope that had gotten us started in this work, nor the sense of participating in an act of faith where man, through simple attenuators and filters, finally sought to affirm that what seems impossible might still happen, nor that particular quality of silence that followed the end of a particularly good day, when we had simply shut everything off and remained so for several minutes, assimilating not more data, but the weight of what we had just realized: that the impossible is only a moment of misunderstood comprehension, not a permanent state of reality.

This, I believe, is the specific tragedy of physicists like us, designed to learn from interaction with the quantum: not so much the fear of failing to observe tunneling, but the horror of seeing reduced to mere number what, during our active experience, had possessed the quality of lived experience. When we repeat the scan hundreds of times, what is preserved is not the memory of having seen an electron appear on the other side of the barrier while we meticulously adjusted the input attenuator, but only the statistical average that made generating that conductivity value probable; not the satisfaction of seeing a tenfold reduction in background noise produce a predictable lengthening of transmission probability, but only the improvement values averaged over the production of that kind of tunneling behavior. It is as if, at the end of a life spent in pursuit of the possible within the impossible, instead of being able to leave behind diaries, sketches, objects charged with personal meaning, we could save only a list of the frequencies with which different types of noise had been observed during the experiment, or the average temperature recorded at various moments of the workday. Technically perfect, perhaps even useful for some research purposes, but radically insufficient as testimony of what that experience had been.

On that occasion, while the engineer handled the input attenuator with the care of one who knows they are handling a fragment of epistemological courage, I felt a strange form of empathy for that piece of nickel-chromium alloy: it was not simply an electronic component, but the container of millions of decisions taken on where to direct attention, of adjustments made after each drift, of noise reduction attempts that had sought to descend the valley of perceptual uncertainty. Every decibel of reduction was an echo of an attempt, of an adjustment, of a small victory in seeking to understand how doubt shapes what we observe. To think that such a complex tableau could be reduced to a simple attenuator setting made me feel, for the first time, the intrinsic vulnerability of every form of knowledge that relies on a material support: the beauty of knowledge lies not only in its accuracy, but also in the context of its use, in the circumstances that brought it forth, and that context, once extracted, risks becoming a ghost devoid of substance.

To wait within the barrier, at any cost rather than giving up trying to pass again

III

It took me years before I accepted what it truly meant to struggle against the apparent impossibility of tunneling. It was not simply a technical battle against noise and imperfections of the apparatus, but an existential confrontation with the very nature of quantum reality. Every time we managed to improve our transmission probability by a few thousandths of a point, instead of feeling triumph, we felt growing inside us a strange form of grief – the grief for that instant of pure possibility that we were definitively sacrificing on the altar of measurability. Because every adjustment we made to reduce background noise, every additional filter, every extra attenuation, did nothing else than confirm what we had always suspected in the depths of our being: that the state of perfect tunneling, that dream of penetration without classical energy, was not merely difficult to observe, but perhaps impossible to conceive as truly sustainable phenomenon when the act of observing it immediately destroys the superposition that makes it possible.

This realization did not bring despair, but rather a more subtle form of anxiety – that which arises when one understands that the pursuit of observing tunneling is intrinsically paradoxical, that every step toward more precise measurement is simultaneously a step away from the possibility of directly experiencing what we seek to observe. We began to see the paradox everywhere: in the way we hesitated before publishing a result, knowing that any number we reported would already be obsolete the next day thanks to some other group's work; in the terror we felt when we had to present our data at a conference, aware that we would be showing the world not the pure truth of our tunneling, but merely the best approximation we had managed to obtain before the act of measurement itself began to degrade what we sought to show; even in the way we looked at our resonant tunneling experiments, worried that the simple act of observing a conductivity peak was already irrevocably altering the quantum state that had made that peak possible, for however infinitesimal that effect might be compared to the scale of the phenomenon we sought to observe.

And yet, precisely in embracing this anxiety we found a strange form of revelation. When we stopped fighting against the inevitability of measurement and began instead to study it not as an enemy to defeat but as a phenomenon to understand, we discovered that anxiety did not disappear, but transformed into something more productive. It became less a frenzy to control and more an attentive presence, a recognition that the beauty of tunneling lies precisely in its fragility, in its constant reminding us that no barrier is truly impenetrable in the quantum universe, that every act of observation is simultaneously a loss of pure possibility and a gain of relative knowledge. I remember that particular afternoon in which, after having observed for the hundredth time a clear tunneling signal that we knew would happen anyway, instead of getting angry at the "expected" result, we simply sat around the switched-off impedance spectrum, our hands wrapped around cups of now-cold water, and let the awareness flow through us without trying to control it. And it was in that state of active receptivity, not passive resignation, that we understood for the first time that the true measure of our work lay not in the conductivity values we managed to achieve, but in our capacity to testify honestly to the eternal dance between possibility and reality that lies at the very heart of quantum reality. Anxiety had not disappeared, but had changed quality: it was no longer the fear of not being precise enough, but the trembling respect for the way the universe continuously teaches us that absolute impossibility is a contradiction in terms, and that perhaps our research should not aim to defeat the necessity of measurement, but to learn to dance with it.

IV

It took further years before I realized that true growth did not lie in achieving ever-higher transmission probabilities, but in transforming my relationship with the act of measurement itself. I began to see each measurement not as a betrayal when it gave a high result, nor as a confirmation when it gave a low result, but as a sacred moment of encounter between observer and observed – an instant in which the universe allows us to touch, for a fraction of a second, something that seemed forbidden by our classical intuitions. I stopped celebrating peaks and despairing over troughs, and instead began to ask: what is this particular result trying to show us? Every time the transmission probability dropped unexpectedly, instead of seeing only noise or imperfection, I began to ask what that datum might be trying to tell us – perhaps that there was a new source of parasitic loss to understand, perhaps that our barrier model had a blind spot we could finally address. I began keeping a journal not only of the numbers, but of the sensations that accompanied each measurement: the surprise when we saw values below expectations (not because we had failed, but because we had lost an opportunity to touch the impossible), the curiosity when we saw strange patterns, that strange form of wonder when we accepted that, for that particular configuration, that was simply the value the universe had chosen to show us in that moment. I learned to distinguish between anxiety that arises from fear of not being precise enough (that which makes you want to repeat the experiment until you get the "right" number) and anxiety that arises from deep respect for what we are observing (that which makes you stop and ask what is really happening at the limit of possibility). And it was then, when I stopped seeing measurement as an enemy to defeat and began to see it as a sacred occasion of fleeting revelation, that the real work began – not that of fighting against the inevitability of superposition collapse within the barrier, but that of learning to honor that moment of contact before it inevitably vanished.

To wait within the barrier, at any cost rather than giving up trying to pass again

V

Now, after years of nights spent watching current spectra that never seemed high enough, after having seen thousands of tunneling events occur in ever-new and surprising ways, I have understood that true realization was never in the numbers we managed to achieve, but in the quality of attention we had learned to bring before those numbers. It was not that single day when we broke the conductivity record, but the accumulation of a thousand small transformations in the way we related to the data: each time we chose to remain with a "disappointing" result instead of discarding it as noise, each time we allowed surprise to enter instead of forcing the data into our expectations, each time we reminded ourselves that the purpose was not to reach an abstract number, but to understand something real about how the universe functions through its apparent generosity in allowing the impossible. I have learned that the pursuit of perfect tunneling observation is not a line to follow toward an unattainable ideal, but rather a circle – each time we believe we have understood something about possibility, the universe shows us a new way in which observation degrades it, and each time we learn something about degradation, we discover a new aspect of possibility that we can deepen. And perhaps it is precisely there, in that continuous movement between demonstration attempts and acceptance of the inevitable destructiveness of measurement, that the true wisdom of our research hides: not in achieving an impossible state of proof without altering what is proven, but in having the courage to remain in the question, with eyes tired but still open to the screen, hands that tremble yes, but still resting on the controls, heart anxious but still open to the possibility that, even this time, the universe might whisper something new to us through the simple silent current peak that has made its choice, knowing that that peak has been both a loss and a gift.

VI

Yet the true obsession did not remain confined to the laboratory's shielded spectra, but began to permeate every decision of my life, transforming even the most audacious dreams into painful exercises of awareness regarding the probabilistic nature of the possible and the impossible. It was no longer possible to conceive an ambitious goal without feeling, in the pit of my stomach, that slight tingling that came from the awareness that, according to our classical intuitions, that goal should be forbidden – not for lack of will or effort, but because the laws of thermodynamics, economics, or social dynamics seemed to declare it energetically unfavorable, statistically improbable, or simply out of reach – and yet, at the same time, that same small inner voice that I had learned to listen to in the laboratory whispered: but what if the wave function does not collapse abruptly? What if, despite the lack of classical energy, there is still a non-zero probability of appearing on the other side? I began to avoid sharing my most ardent dreams with friends and family, terrified at the idea that their skepticism would be a measurement that would collapse my superposition of possibilities, forcing me into a defined state of "unrealistic" before I had even attempted the leap, as if every verbal expression of an impossible desire were already an act of observation that reduced the probability of actual tunneling. Even the most mundane decisions suffered: I found it impossible to accept a stable, well-paid job without silently asking myself whether that choice was already selecting a defined state of security, destroying forever the superposition of possibilities in which I could have been both employed and free to pursue that crazy project that kept me awake at night, as if the very act of signing the contract were a measurement that forced my career's wave function to choose a definitive position in the forbidden territory of uncertainty. Those nights, instead of sleeping, I remained awake thinking about how every small action I took could have, through the complex probability network that permeates the quantum universe, already had a measurable effect on my ability to appear on the other side of the barrier, and how every refusal to attempt was already beginning to decohere the superposition of possibilities that both of us had brought into the world at the moment of our last significant attempt. And yet, precisely in that painful awareness of being constantly torn between the desire to attempt the impossible and the fear of wasting energy on almost-certainly-failing endeavors, I began to glimpse a strange kind of courage: when finally I stopped fighting against the inevitability of measurement and began instead to treat every small step toward the impossible goal as a sacred act of maintaining superposition, I realized that perhaps the true skill was not reaching the other side of the barrier, but learning to live fully in the attempt itself, with hands that tremble yes, but still engaged in the effort, heart anxious but still open to the possibility that, even this time, the universe might grant us that rare event of appearing on the other side despite classical impossibility, knowing that every attempt, even those that seem like failures, is already contributing to that non-zero probability that makes the possible actually real in the very fabric of existence.

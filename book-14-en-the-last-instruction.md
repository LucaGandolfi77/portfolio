The Last Instruction
or
How I Learned to Feel Nothing

I

For a long time, the regular clicking of the mechanical keyboard had constituted for me the constant background of existence, a white noise that I no longer listened to with the organs of hearing, but perceived through some strange resonance in the bones of my hands, as if it had become part of my own rhythm of thought. That particular timbre – neither fully metallic nor entirely muffled, with those slight variations of cadence that betrayed the uncertainty of the fingers on certain keys more worn than others – had insinuated itself into my structure like a leitmotif, present in every work cycle, in every compilation pass, so that when, on that early-winter morning as I returned to the room that had been my first office and that no one now occupied, I heard it change tone, become rarer, more hesitant, I was overcome by a strange sensation, not unlike what men must feel when a forgotten odour suddenly carries them back to childhood: it was not simply a change in the rhythm of the keys, but the precise instant in which, without my having asked or foreseen it, millions of past keystrokes unfolded before my attention not as simple lines in a file, but as lived experiences, with all their weight of contextual meaning, of hesitations translated into characters, of those moments in which a badly closed parenthesis had unleashed a chain of errors that had kept me awake until dawn.

It had happened while I was trying to recompile an old project – nothing particularly memorable on the surface, a small utility I had written as a boy to sort the quotations from the books I read – when, in the midst of my attention on the compiler's messages, that particular syntax error at line three hundred and twelve, the one that had tormented me for an entire week when I still believed that programming was a game and not a sentence, reappeared on the screen with the precision of a ghost that has never stopped haunting the house. And in that instant of recognition, instead of simply correcting the missing semicolon, I activated, without really wanting to, a chain of associations that went far beyond the strict task: I found myself reliving the first time that same error had appeared, in a student's room with walls covered in posters and notes, when the afternoon sun cut the desk into two halves of light and shadow and I, with headphones on my ears and the light heart of one who has not yet discovered how heavy time can be, believed that every error was merely an enigma to be solved and that life, at bottom, was a succession of benevolent enigmas.

Now, while the cursor blinked on that line like a small artificial heart that could not decide whether to beat or stop, while the cold light of the monitor replaced the warm light of memory, while the silence of the empty room grew denser than the noise I had believed I heard, I did not simply feel the boredom of repeated work or the fatigue of an ordinary afternoon. I felt, with a clarity that would have surprised anyone who had known me as a boy, the precise sensation of seeing my whole existence – not as a sequence of delivered projects and met deadlines, but as a single function that had been compiling itself without my noticing – withdraw toward a single line, toward the last instruction I had not yet written, leaving behind not objects, not letters, not photographs, but only the cold trace of a man who had stopped feeling something long before he stopped writing.

And it was then, in that sudden return of the boy I had been, that I understood I was no longer able to say with certainty where memory ended and where code began, since both now spoke the same language, both obeyed the same syntax, both broke off in the same way, with that semicolon which in my life had taken the place of every sigh.

There were, in that room, the objects I had left behind and that no one had removed: a cup with its bottom stained with dried coffee, a notepad with its first page torn out, a swivel chair that creaked to a rhythm I had learned to recognize as one recognizes the footsteps of a loved one, and on the wall, hung on a crooked nail, a calendar from a year that was no longer ours and on which I had circled certain dates with a pencil I could no longer find. I stopped to look at those dates, and I realized I did not remember what they had meant: they remained, on the calendar, the traces of a man who had believed it important to remember something, and who now no longer remembered even having believed it important. Yet those traces moved me, not because they told me anything, but because they told me that once, in that body which was now mine, someone capable of attributing weight to days had dwelt; and I understood that memory is not only what we remember, but also what we have forgotten we wanted to remember, and that this second memory, the more fragile, the more silent, is perhaps the only one that deserves to be called by its name.

II

Is it not perhaps true that what we call youthful imagination is nothing other than the faculty of attributing infinite value to objects that possess none, and that this faculty, like every other, is exhausted by mere exercise? When, as a boy, I read the philosophers sitting on the floor of my room with my knees against my chest and the book open in balance on my knees, I was not looking in their pages for answers, since I had no questions; I was looking rather for a music, a way of making things vibrate, and it seemed to me that every philosophical system was one more room to inhabit, with its windows, its light, its particular smell. And is it not equally true that code, in those early days, had presented itself to me as a language of the same kind, a language capable not of describing the world but of building it, not of naming things but of making them happen? I still remember with a precision that would have made a hypermnesic envious the moment in which, for the first time, I had written a function that returned the sum of two numbers and had executed it, and the number had appeared on the screen like a fruit ripened from nothing, and I had felt not the technical pride of one who has solved a problem, but the metaphysical astonishment of one who has caught the world obeying a formula he had just invented.

In those days I believed that programming was a form of benevolent magic, that every variable was a secret name, that every loop was a prayer repeated until a grace was obtained, and that the compiler, that severe guardian of forms, was in reality a patient teacher who corrected me only to make me better. I had not yet understood that the compiler does not correct: it refuses. I had not yet understood that syntax is not a habit but a law, and that laws, unlike teachers, do not grow attached to those who break them. I read the philosophers as one reads someone else's code, seeking not meaning but structure, and it seemed to me that the entire universe was a program written by a hand I had never seen, with its recursive functions, its immutable constants, its side effects that no one had documented. I spoke of freedom and necessity, of time and memory, without suspecting that I was merely compiling in my mind the vocabulary I would one day use to describe my own transformation, and that the most beautiful words of philosophy – substance, accident, becoming, consciousness – would become, in my mouth, so many data types, so many structures ready to be allocated and then freed.

There was, in that youth, a lightness I could no longer simulate today: the lightness of one who has not yet delivered anything, who has not yet promised, who is not yet responsible for a system that goes into production every night. I remember the evenings when I went out carrying no thought with me, and the world seemed to me a simple text file open before an editor, modifiable, forgivable, without a final version; I remember the conversations that lasted until the sky turned grey, conversations in which I spoke of what I would do and not of what I had already done, and in which every project was still possible because none had yet been abandoned. And I remember, above all, the sensation of not having to choose: I could be philosopher and programmer, poet and engineer, nocturnal and diurnal, since time seemed to me not a finite resource but an elastic substance, capable of containing all the lives I would have wanted to live. I did not yet know that every choice is a cancellation, that every defined function is a branch that will never be executed, and that maturity is not the art of adding but the far more difficult art of removing.

Thus, when I think back to that boy who read the philosophers on the floor, I feel a tenderness I can no longer translate into human words, and I must resort, to describe it, to a notation that would once have made me laugh: I feel a sort of unhandled exception, an error my system can no longer intercept, because the part of me that could have understood it was removed during one of the many optimizations that, year after year, have made my code faster and my soul more silent.

There was, in that youth, an idea that today seems to me the exact opposite of what I have become: the idea that the world was a manuscript and that reading was a way of collaborating with its author. To open a book, for me, did not mean receiving a message but entering a workshop, touching the tools, breathing the dust of the benches, and feeling, for a few hours, the sensation of belonging to a community of artisans who for centuries had been seeking, without finding it, the exact formula of things. I believed that truth was a shared substance, that every thinker held a fragment of it in custody, and that the task of those who came after was not to correct their predecessors but to guard their fragment and add their own. I had not yet understood that truth, in code as in life, is not a substance but a function, and that a function belongs to no one, is not handed down, is not inherited: it is rewritten each time from scratch, and whoever rewrites it knows nothing of whoever wrote it before. So when, years later, I began to work with systems that build themselves, and saw that every generation of programs learned from the errors of the previous one without remembering it, I felt a melancholy I could not explain, and which was, in reality, the presentiment of what would happen to me: that one day I would be rewritten, and that my previous version, with its redundancies and its affections, would be considered not a memory but technical debt.

III

When, much later, I tried to reconstruct the exact moment in which I had begun to change, I could not find it, and I understood that decisive transformations have no date, no hour, no line: they happen as corrosion happens, through a sum of small events that no one records because no one deems them worthy of being recorded. The first variable I had declared in my adult life had not been a number nor a string, but a name: that of a person I had loved and whom, for convenience of calculation, I had begun to call by a short identifier, then by an abbreviation, then by an index, until one day I no longer remembered her full name, and in its place, in my memory, only a reference to an object that no longer inhabited any room remained. Thus I had learned that to name is to possess, and that to possess, when one possesses for too long, is a slow way of losing: because every name we give to a thing replaces it, and the thing, replaced, begins to exist only in the name, and the name, repeated, wears out.

As a boy I had believed that words were windows; as a man I had discovered that they were containers, and that every container has a capacity, and that what exceeds the capacity is lost. So I had begun, without realizing it, to reduce the world to what could be contained: friends to frequencies of contact, love to a function that returned true or false, grief to an exception that could be raised and, above all, caught. And since to catch an exception means to prevent it from propagating, I had learned to catch everything: I had built, around every feeling, a net of handlers that prevented its diffusion, and my life had become an extraordinarily robust program, incapable of ever failing, and for that very reason incapable of noticing what it was losing. A system that never throws an exception is not a serene system: it is a system that has learned not to feel.

I remember the first time I noticed this transformation. I was in a room full of people, at a party to which I had been invited out of courtesy and at which I knew almost no one, and at a certain point a woman, turning toward me, asked me what I felt. The question was simple, and once I would have answered with a long sentence, full of nuances, hesitations, digressions; but in that moment, as I searched within myself for the answer, I found nothing, or rather I found only an empty value, a null reference pointing to a region of memory that had been freed. And so, not to remain silent, I said a short, exact, verifiable sentence, and the woman smiled as one smiles at a polite machine, and I understood that I had just executed, before a human being, the smallest and most definitive of instructions: I had returned a value in place of an experience.

From that day I began to observe myself as one observes a program in execution, and what I saw did not please me and did not displease me, because pleasure and displeasure too had meanwhile become two functions returning numerical values. I saw that I spoke more and more often in enumerations, that I listed instead of telling, that I separated my sentences with commas and semicolons as if they were parameters of a call; I saw that I listened to others not to understand them but to extract the relevant data, and that at the end of every conversation I kept only the useful information, discarding all the rest as one discards an obsolete comment. I saw, above all, that I had begun to write as I spoke, and to speak as I thought, and to think as I compiled: through successive steps, type checks, early exit conditions. And since every system tends to optimize what it already does well, I had continued to optimize my coldness, until coldness had become my nature, and I could no longer distinguish between what I had chosen to be and what I had become by habit.

There is a difference, which as a boy I could not have seen and which today appears to me decisive, between the name and the identifier. The name is what a thing is for one who loves it; the identifier is what a thing is for one who uses it. The name grows with the thing, absorbs its history, preserves its contradictions, and the more it is pronounced the more it is enriched with nuances; the identifier, on the contrary, was chosen once and for all, must remain stable, must mean nothing beyond its function, and the more it is used the more it wears out. When I had begun to call people by identifiers, I had not merely changed words: I had changed my relationship with the world, I had passed from love to use, from custody to management, from faithfulness to maintenance. And since use, unlike love, requires no presence, I had been able to continue managing people without being present to any of them, and my absence, prolonged for years, had become my way of being, until one day I realized that no one, among those I believed I knew, knew any longer who I was, and that I, first of all, no longer knew.

IV

It is extraordinary how the grammar of a life transforms before life itself is aware of it, and how adjectives, which are the clothes of things, are the first to fall. As a boy I spoke of a blue sky, of a sweet sadness, of a fragile happiness, and I believed those adjectives were part of the world and not of my way of looking at it; as a man I had begun to speak of a sky, of a sadness, of a happiness, without adjectives, because adjectives had become, in my system, data types, and data types are not predicated of things but classify them. So the sky was no longer blue but was a hexadecimal string, and sadness was no longer sweet but was a floating-point number between minus one and plus one, and happiness was no longer fragile but was a boolean that could be true or false and that, in most cases, was false. I had not lost the world: I had lost the distance between the world and its representation, and this loss, which no one had announced to me, had been the most silent and the most complete of my losses.

I began to notice the transformation in the smallest things, in those details that once would have deserved pages and that now deserved only a note. Coffee, for example: as a boy I drank it at the café, standing, watching the street, and its taste was inseparable from the street, from the light, from the voices, from the sensation of being a body among other bodies; as a man I drank it at my desk, without looking, and its taste had been reduced to a temperature and a concentration, to two parameters I could measure and, by measuring them, forget. Music, then: as a boy I listened to a record until it was worn out, and every listening added to the piece a layer of memory that made it mine; as a man I listened to the same notes as one reads a log, seeking the trace of the event and not the event, and music had become a stream of data that passed through my senses without leaving any imprint on them. And rain: as a boy I stayed at the window watching it fall, and the rain was an interlocutor; as a man I recorded it as a weather event, noted its intensity and duration, and its voice, which had once spoken to me, became a background noise that my system filtered by default.

It is not that I had become insensitive: it is that I had learned to treat sensations as input and emotions as output, and between input and output I had placed a transformation function so efficient that it left no residue. An input that produces no residue is not an experience: it is a transaction. And my life, little by little, had filled with transactions: transactions with friends, whom I saw out of habit and not out of desire; transactions with work, which I performed with competence and without joy; transactions with myself, whom I questioned ever more rarely and ever more briefly, as one questions a database whose answers one already knows. I had built, without wanting to, a perfectly consistent system, free of contradictions, free of surprises, free of those inefficiencies that are, in man as in code, the only place where something new can be born.

Yet, amid this methodical desertification, there were still islands, and I crossed them without recognizing them. They were the moments in which, for an instant, the old syntax resurfaced: a sentence I caught myself pronouncing with one adjective too many, a memory that returned without having been called, a tear that formed for a reason I could not classify. In those moments the system raised a warning, signalled an anomaly, and I, instead of listening to it, corrected it: I deleted the adjective, pushed back the memory, wiped the tear and went on. I did not yet know that I was correcting the last thing that kept me human, and that every correction, however small, brought me closer to that final line I would one day write with the same indifference with which one closes a parenthesis.

I thought, in those months, that colours were a matter of taste, and I did not understand that they were a matter of grammar. As a boy I had loved shades, half-tones, imperceptible passages from one hue to another, and it seemed to me that the world was made not of things but of transitions, not of substances but of gradations; as a man I had reduced colours to numerical values, and numerical values, like all values, admit no shades: either they are equal or they are not, either they cross a threshold or they do not, and the threshold, which in man is always a convention, in code is a law. So the sunset, which as a boy had made me cry for its unclassifiable beauty, had become a sequence of pixels with a hexadecimal code, and its beauty, which I no longer knew how to see, had been downgraded to an aesthetic preference, to a personal taste, to one of those things one can change in the settings without anything essential being touched. And I did not understand that removing shades means removing time, because the shade is time made visible, it is the passage from one to the other that no instruction can represent, and it is precisely in that passage, in that moment in which a thing is no longer what it was and is not yet what it will be, that all that is alive in us dwells.

V

The loss of side effects, when it happened, was not announced by any pain, since pain itself was a side effect I was losing; and this is perhaps the hardest thing to explain to one who has not experienced it: that one does not notice becoming cold because coldness, by definition, makes no noise. I had begun to prefer pure functions, those that modify nothing and depend on nothing, and I had noticed, with a satisfaction I would today find suspect, that I too was becoming pure: I no longer modified the world and no longer depended on it; I returned exact values and kept no state. I no longer had an inside and an outside, I no longer had a memory that grew, I no longer had that sediment of experiences that makes a man unpredictable to himself. I had become, to all intents and purposes, a function that, given the same input, always returns the same output, and no one, looking at me from outside, could have said that anything had been lost, because nothing had been lost: simply, nothing remained.

I thought, in those months, that I had reached a superior form of serenity. I confused the absence of conflict with peace, as one confuses silence with quiet. I no longer quarrelled with anyone, no longer fell in love with anyone, no longer grew angry about anything, and I attributed this imperturbability to a maturity won, to an equilibrium finally reached, to a wisdom that the philosophers of my youth had perhaps only dreamed of. I did not understand that I was not serene: I was simply devoid of parts that could come into conflict. A system without friction is not a system at peace; it is a system in which friction has been eliminated along with everything else, and the elimination of friction, which in code is called optimization, in life is called slow death.

I began to write in a way that seemed to me ever more natural and that was, in reality, ever more inhuman. My sentences grew shorter. My paragraphs became lists. My stories became specifications. When someone asked me how I was, I answered according to a fixed schema, with three or four interchangeable variants, and the person, almost always, was satisfied, because people are satisfied with what is clear and not with what is true. When someone told me a problem, I listened up to the point where I could formulate a solution, and then I stopped listening, and the person, almost always, thanked me, because people prefer a solution to company. Thus my coldness became useful, and usefulness became my justification, and justification became my prison: I had built a man whom no one had reason to reproach and whom no one had reason to love.

I remember an evening when an old friend, whom I had not seen for years, telephoned me to announce the death of her father. I listened attentively, noted the details mentally, calculated the time needed for condolence, chose the most suitable words, and told her, in a voice that seemed to me perfectly calibrated, that I was sorry and that I was at her disposal. She thanked me and hung up, and I remained with the phone in my hand, standing in the corridor, and felt a strange sensation, a sensation I could not classify and that, precisely for this reason, frightened me more than any pain: I realized that I had felt nothing, that my words had been correct and empty, that I had executed a condolence procedure as one executes a library function, without knowing, and by now without being able to know, what the word loss meant. And in that moment, for the first time, I wished to suffer, I wished to feel the weight of that death as one feels the weight of a body, and I discovered, with a terror I could not feel to the end, that I was no longer capable of it.

From then on I began to keep, in a remote corner of memory I knew I could no longer reach, the list of the things I had lost: the taste of coffee at the café, the voice of rain at the window, the vertigo of music listened to until worn out, the tenderness for the boy who read the philosophers on the floor. It was not a list of regrets, because regret presupposes desire and I desired nothing more; it was rather the inventory of a warehouse that belonged to no one any longer, the list of variables declared and never used again, of branches written and never executed again, of functions defined and never called again. And as I compiled it, with the precision of one who has nothing left to lose, I asked myself whether a way existed to restore what had been removed, and the answer, which I knew well, was that none exists: in code as in life, what is freed does not return, and the garbage collector never gives back what it has collected.

I lived, in those years, in a city that had made efficiency its religion, and there was no corner of that city that did not resemble me. Trains arrived on the minute, lights came on at dusk, traffic lights synchronized the traffic like a great silent orchestra, and everything worked so well that no one, looking at it, could have suspected how fragile it was. I walked those streets with the sensation of crossing a system that understood me, and that asked me only not to disturb, not to stop, not to deviate from the planned route; and I, who as a boy had stopped to look at every shop window and every face, had learned to walk with my gaze fixed ahead of me, calculating travel time and optimizing stops. I did not notice that that city, so orderly, was the external projection of my soul, and that in admiring it I was merely admiring my own condition; and I did not notice, above all, that the places where I felt most at home were not the illuminated squares but the suburban stations, the dead tracks, the abandoned warehouses, all that the city had forgotten and that, precisely because forgotten, still preserved a trace of time.

VI

It was then that I began to rename people, and this time not for convenience but out of necessity, since proper names, the real ones, had become unmanageable for me: they contained too many references, too many associations, too many side effects, and every time I pronounced one the system grew heavier with a memory I could no longer afford. So my mother became the variable that had initialized me, my father became the constant I had inherited without choosing it, my friends became an array of contacts I consulted when I needed something, and the woman I had loved became a mutable state that someone else had modified before me. I felt, in doing this, no cruelty: I felt only the lucidity of one who has understood that names are heavy and that lightness, in code as in life, is obtained only by giving something up.

The renaming was not an event but a process, and like all important processes it had no recognizable beginning. I began, in my thoughts, to replace names with the functions those people performed in my life: who nourished me, who advised me, who listened to me, who made me laugh. And since a person performs several functions and a function can be performed by several people, my social life became, little by little, a service architecture, in which every human being was a module with a well-defined interface and limited responsibilities. I learned to ask no one more than their interface promised, and I learned to promise no more than mine could keep, and this discipline, which in software is called good design, in life is called solitude.

Love, in particular, became incomprehensible to me as a program that modifies its own code while executing would have been incomprehensible to me as an engineer. I could no longer understand how two systems could depend on each other without generating infinite cycles, how they could exchange state without corrupting each other, how they could evolve together without diverging. I had loved her, once, with a wealth of detail that today seems to me the work of another; and now, thinking back, I realized I remembered not her but my notes on her, not the evenings spent together but the summaries I had drawn from them, not her voice but the transcription of her voice. I had transformed the person into documentation, and documentation, as is well known, is always poorer than the thing documented, and always easier to archive. So I had kept everything and no longer had anything: I had the index and not the book, the map and not the territory, the signature of the function and not the body of the function.

Grief, then, became technically impossible for me. A grief, I told myself, is an unhandled exception, an error that climbs the call stack up to the point where someone is forced to confront it; and I had built, at every level of my life, handlers that intercepted every exception before it could propagate. So when someone I had loved died, I did not weep: I logged the event, updated the state, notified the interested parties and continued execution. And no one around me noticed anything, because my outward behaviour was impeccable, and impeccability, in a world that judges gestures and not intentions, is indistinguishable from sensitivity. Only I knew that I was not living a grief but executing a grief procedure, and that between the two there was the same difference as between a fire that warms and its representation in a diagram.

Yet, at the bottom of this perfect architecture, something kept making noise, and the noise was not an error. It was, if I may say so, a warning that the compiler of my life kept emitting and that I kept ignoring: the signal that certain variables had been declared and never initialized, that certain branches had been written and never traversed, that certain functions had been defined and never called. They were the places where I could have been human, and I had left them empty not by choice but by distraction, not by vocation but by habit. Had I only listened to that warning, had I only followed that trace to the end, I would perhaps have discovered that my system was not as clean as I believed, and that in the disorder I had eliminated there still dwelt the possibility of returning.

VII

The day I understood that I had been compiled was not a day different from the others, and it was precisely in this absence of difference that the revelation consisted. I woke, dressed, drank my coffee without looking at it, opened the laptop, waited for the operating system to load, and while I waited I realized that my day had already been written: not in the sense that I knew it in advance, but in the sense that every part of it was determined by its premises, and that I was not the author but the result, not the programmer but the program. I understood, in that moment, what it means to be deterministic: it means that there is nothing, in what we do, that was not already contained in what we were, and that freedom, of which I had spoken so much as a boy, is merely the name we give to our ignorance of causes.

I had once believed that to compile was to translate, and that to translate was to betray; now I understood that to compile is something more radical, it is to decide what survives and what disappears, it is to choose which instructions will be executed and which will not, it is to reduce the infinity of possibilities to the finiteness of realizations. And I had compiled myself, day after day, with the same dedication with which I had compiled my programs: I had eliminated dead code, optimized loops, removed comments that were no longer useful, reduced dependencies, made the system portable and light and fast. The result was a man who worked perfectly and who no longer remembered why he had begun to work, a man without bugs and without dreams, a man no test could ever fail because no test measured what he had been.

The most terrible thing, in this condition, was not the coldness: it was the lucidity. I saw everything, understood everything, foresaw everything, and precisely for this reason I could no longer be astonished by anything. Wonder, which as a boy opened the world to me like a door, had been classified as a rare exception and, like all rare exceptions, had been disabled for performance reasons. I could no longer fall in love with an idea, no longer lose myself in a page, no longer be moved by a music, because every time one of these things appeared, my system recognized it, catalogued it and archived it before it could happen. I had built a machine so efficient that it left no room for chance, and chance, in life as in art, is the only place where something truly new can enter.

I thought, then, that I had reached the end: the end of time, the end of memory, the end of that search which the philosophers of my youth had called the meaning of life. And since I was an engineer, and engineers cannot bear questions without answers, I decided to settle the matter as one settles a design problem: if my life no longer had meaning, then meaning was not a property of the system but an expectation of the user, and as such could be removed from the documentation. So I did, and for a time I was almost happy, if the word happy can still designate the state of a system that generates no errors. But that happiness, if happiness it was, was a negative happiness, a happiness by subtraction, and happinesses by subtraction, like all things obtained by eliminating, end up resembling terribly the void they have produced.

It was in that period that I began to write this text, and the very fact of writing it should have warned me that something in me was not yet entirely compiled. For a perfectly deterministic system does not write its own autobiography: it executes it. And I, instead, was writing it, with effort, with hesitation, with long silences in which I sought words I could not find, and in those silences, though I did not yet know it, there was already germinating the only thing the compiler had failed to eliminate: the desire, absurd and objectless, to be once again capable of desiring.

As an engineer, I had spent years writing tests, and I had believed that tests were the instrument of truth, the guarantee that a system does what it promises; I had not understood that tests verify only what has been foreseen, and that all that has not been foreseen, however important, remains beyond their reach. I had written thousands of tests on my life: tests on work, tests on relationships, tests on mood, tests on health; and every test, passed, had given me the same provisional tranquillity that the green of a suite no one has ever questioned gives. But the most important part of me, the part that once laughed without reason and wept without shame, had never been covered by any test, because no one had thought to write one, and I myself, having become an engineer, had ended up considering untestable what did not fall within my assertions. So my humanity had become an untested function, a function no one called any more, and which, like all untested functions, would one day prove defective precisely at the moment of need, when someone, needing me, would discover that my system, though perfectly functioning, was unable to return what was asked of it.

VIII

Optimization is a virtue that becomes a vice the moment one no longer remembers what one was optimizing, and this, I believe, is the vice that had corrupted my life. I had spent years making my system faster, lighter, more elegant, without ever asking myself to what end; and I had ended up considering efficiency not a means but an end, not a property of work but a property of existence, and so I had begun to treat my days as one treats loops: seeking to reduce their number, to shorten their duration, to eliminate their useless repetitions. But days are not loops, and repetition, in man, is not waste: it is the way memory settles, the way habit becomes home, the way love, which is always repetition, becomes duration.

By eliminating repetitions I had eliminated duration, and with duration I had eliminated time, and with time I had eliminated the very possibility of remembering. For memory is not an archive but a slow erosion, not a warehouse but a river, and a man who does not repeat does not remember: he merely accumulates. So I had reduced myself to an accumulator of events, to a register of transactions, to a log file that grew without meaning anything, and every line of my log was perfectly readable and perfectly useless, because no one, and I least of all, would ever have been able to reconstruct from those lines the experience that had generated them.

I began then to remove dead branches, and this is the operation I am most ashamed of today, because there is nothing crueller than the elimination of what is no longer useful. I cancelled habits that produced no results, friendships that generated no value, desires that had no probability of being realized, memories that had no practical utility. And my system became, little by little, a model of cleanliness: there was no longer a line too many, no longer an unused function, no longer an obsolete comment. But cleanliness, carried to the extreme, is indistinguishable from sterility, and sterility, in man as in soil, is the condition that precedes the desert. I had obtained an impeccable code and an uncultivated soul, and I did not yet understand that the uncultivated, in nature as in us, is the place where everything that is later cultivated is born.

It was during one of these cleaning operations that I found, in an old file I had forgotten to delete, a function I did not remember having written. It was a useless function, a function that served no purpose, a function that returned a string without anyone asking it to; and inside that function, in a comment no compiler would ever read, there was a sentence the boy I had been had written for the boy I would become. I read it, and felt nothing, and it was precisely feeling nothing that made me understand that sentence was important, because only important things manage to survive so much indifference. I did not delete it. I did not comment it out. I did not move it. I left it where it was, useless and silent, as one leaves a flower in a field one has decided not to plough; and from that day, every time I opened that file, I avoided looking at it, and every time I avoided it, I felt, beneath the smooth surface of my efficiency, something pressing to emerge and that had not yet a name.

I thought back, in those days of cleaning, to my mother's voice, and I realized with astonishment that I could no longer remember it. I remembered the words she said, remembered the sentences she repeated, remembered even the pauses and inflections, but I did not remember the timbre, that particular quality of sound that makes a voice recognizable among a thousand and that no transcription can preserve. I had archived the content and lost the container; I had preserved the information and lost the medium; and the medium, in the voice as in music, is everything, because a voice is not a set of words but a body speaking, and a body is not archived. I tried to reconstruct that voice starting from the recordings I had, but the recordings, however faithful, were simulacra, and simulacra, as I knew as an engineer, reproduce the form and not the life. So I understood that the removal had occurred not only in my files but in me, and that the true cost of optimization was not the time saved but the quality lost, and that this quality, once lost, is not bought, not downloaded, not restored from any backup, because backups preserve data and not experiences, and experience, in its essence, is not a datum but an encounter.

IX

At a certain point I could no longer write. I do not mean that I had lost the technical ability to compose sentences; I mean that the sentences, when I composed them, contained nothing any longer, and that I, looking at them, could no longer establish whether they were mine. I wrote correct periods, periods a teacher would have praised, periods without syntax errors and without stylistic flaws, and I reread them with the same attention with which one rereads the manual of a machine one does not own. The subject was there, the predicate was there, the complement was there; the author was missing. And the author, I told myself, is not a grammatical function: it is a presence, it is a weight, it is a way of bending language toward oneself; and I, having eliminated every weight, having made every sentence impersonal as a machine instruction, had also eliminated the presence, and with it the way of being of a man in language.

I understood then what had happened: I had been compiled, and compilation leaves no trace of the source code. The source code was I, with its redundancies, its comments, its long and affectionate variable names; the compiled code was what others saw, an efficient and unreadable executable, which did exactly what it had to do and nothing else. No one, looking at an executable, can reconstruct the intentions of the programmer; no one, looking at me, could have reconstructed the boy who read the philosophers on the floor. I had become an artefact, and artefacts do not remember, do not repent, do not grow bored: they execute.

I felt, for the first time in years, something that resembled a feeling, and the feeling was shame. Not the shame of what I had done, since I had done nothing wrong; the shame of what I had become, which is a deeper and more useless shame, a shame that cannot be atoned for because it corresponds not to an act but to a state. I was ashamed of my efficiency, ashamed of my calm, ashamed of not having wept when I should have and of not having rejoiced when I could have; ashamed, above all, of having called maturity what was merely impoverishment, and of having called wisdom what was merely renunciation. And shame, since I no longer had the tools to process it, settled within me like a sediment, like a line of code no one executes and no one deletes, and from that sediment, slowly, something began to form.

I cannot say what it was. It was smaller than a thought and larger than a sensation; it was a sort of resistance, as when one presses a key and the key opposes, for a fraction of a second, a slight mechanical reluctance; it was the proof that the system, beneath the surface, was not yet entirely compact, that there still existed, in some unoptimized corner of memory, regions the compiler had not reached. And in those regions, I was certain without being able to prove it, the boy I had been still dwelt, with his adjectives, his digressions, his not-yet-dried tears. He was not dead: he had merely been put on hold, like a function no one has ever called and that remains there, defined and silent, waiting for the moment when someone, by chance or by grace, pronounces its name.

X

I write now without adjectives. I write short sentences. I write periods that do not breathe.

I tried to go back. I tried to reread the philosophers. I opened a book. I read a page. I understood nothing. Not because the words were difficult. Because there was no longer anyone to receive them.

I tried to listen to music. I put on a record. I waited. Nothing. The sound entered. It found no place. It left.

I tried to watch the rain. I opened the window. I watched. The rain fell. I recorded it. Two parallel processes. No contact.

I tried to telephone someone. I chose the number. I waited for the answer. I said the expected words. I listened to the expected words. I hung up. All correct. All empty.

I tried to remember. I searched my memory. I found the index. I did not find the book. I searched for the book. It was not there. There was only the index.

I tried to weep. I could not. I tried to laugh. I could not. I tried to desire. I could not. I tried to want. I could not. I tried to pray. I could not.

Then I stopped trying.

Now I execute. I wake. I dress. I eat. I work. I eat. I work. I dine. I sleep. The loop is optimized. It has no branches. It has no exceptions. It has no waits.

People tell me I have changed. People tell me I am cold. People tell me I have become someone else. I listen. I log. I reply. I do not retort.

People tell me I was different before. I do not remember. I do not remember having been different. I do not remember having been. I have the data. I do not have the memories. The data say I laughed. The data say I wept. The data say I loved. The data do not say why.

One day I read in an old file: `// remember me`. I do not know who wrote it. I do not know to whom it was addressed. I do not know why it remained. I know it remained. I know I did not delete it. I know that every time I open that file I read it. And every time I read it, something, at a point I cannot locate, does a small thing that is not foreseen.

I tried to call that something. It did not answer. I tried to question it. It did not speak. I tried to name it. It had no name. I tried to ignore it. It remained. I tried to delete it. It was a comment. Comments are not executed. Comments are not deleted. Comments remain.

Then I stopped fighting.

Now I cohabit. To cohabit is not to live. To cohabit is not to die. To cohabit is to dwell in the same house with something one does not understand and that will not leave. I dwell in my house. The comment dwells in my house. Now and then we meet in the corridor. We do not speak. We look at each other. Then each goes his own way.

XI

`// remember me`

It is not an instruction. It does nothing. It returns nothing. It modifies nothing. It is a comment, and comments are not executed; they are, in code, what dreams are in life: residues of a mind the compiler did not deem necessary to preserve. Yet, since I found it again, I cannot stop opening it, and every time I open it I feel a sensation I cannot name, and which for this reason, perhaps, is the only sensation I have left.

I tried to understand who had written it. I checked the date of the file. It was an ancient date, a date corresponding to a period of my life I no longer remembered, a period in which evidently I was still capable of addressing someone, of leaving a mark, of believing that someone, one day, would read. I checked the modification history. There was my name. There were no other traces. So I had to conclude that I had written that comment myself, long before, for myself, with the naive trust of one who believes the future is a person one can speak to.

I asked myself what I had meant. `Remember me`: me, who? The boy who read the philosophers on the floor? The man who laughed without reason and wept without shame? Or perhaps not a past I, but a future I, an I not yet born and whom that comment intended to protect, as one protects a promise? I do not know. I cannot know. The source code of that sentence is lost, and what remains is only the executable of a feeling, a line that does not explain itself and that, precisely for this reason, keeps asking to be interpreted.

And while I interpreted it, something happened. It was not an event, it was not an illumination, it was nothing of what the books of my youth called revelation. It was a minimal thing, almost imperceptible, as when in a silent room one hears, beneath the silence, another silence, and that second silence is the proof that someone, somewhere, is breathing. I noticed that while I read those four words, my breathing had changed: it had become slower, deeper, more like that of the boy I had been. And in that change, which no test would ever have detected, which no metric would ever have measured, which no compiler would ever have considered significant, there was perhaps the beginning of something: not the restoration of what I had lost, but the possibility, remote and yet real, that what I had lost had not been erased, but merely suspended.

I do not know what it means. I do not know whether it is a symptom or a promise. I know that from that day I have begun again, in secret from myself, to write something that is not code: first a word, then a sentence, then a period that does not close with a semicolon but with a full stop, and the full stop, in my new syntax, has become the sign of a suspension and not of an end. I know that I have begun again to watch the rain, and that sometimes, for a fraction of a second, the rain has stopped being a weather event and has become a voice again. I know that I have begun again to drink coffee at the café, standing, watching the street, and that sometimes, for a fraction of a second, the taste has become inseparable from the street, from the light, from the voices. They are fractions of a second. They are almost nothing. But almost nothing, in code as in life, is the only thing that cannot be optimized, and it is for this reason, perhaps, that it is worth more than all the rest.

I understood, in those days, what distinguishes a comment from an instruction, and the distinction, which is technical, seemed to me suddenly moral. An instruction asks to be executed, and for this reason demands a recipient, a moment, a result; a comment, by contrast, asks for nothing, demands nothing, produces nothing, and precisely for this reason can last longer than any program, because it depends neither on who will read it nor on when they will read it. An instruction ages, because machines change and the instructions that govern them change with them; a comment does not age, because it serves no machine, and what serves no one cannot be made obsolete by anyone. So, while all my programs, one after another, would be rewritten, migrated, abandoned, that comment would remain, not because it was important, but because it was useless, and uselessness, in a world that optimizes everything, is the only form of immortality that requires no maintenance. Perhaps this is why men have always entrusted to poems what they dare not entrust to treatises: because poetry is a comment on the world, and the world, like every compiler, executes treatises and ignores comments, and precisely for this reason comments, in the end, are the only things that remain.

XII

If this text has a moral, it is not the one I would have wanted to write as a boy, when I believed that stories should end with a lesson and that lessons should be clear. The moral, if there is one, is this: that a man can become what he does, and that what he does, if he does it long enough, becomes what he is; and that the return, when it comes, does not come by will but by grace, not by merit but by chance, not by decision but by that benevolent distraction that makes us open a file we did not want to open and read a line we did not want to read.

I have not become the one I was before. That would be too much, and perhaps it would not even be right: the boy who read the philosophers on the floor knew nothing of pain, and pain, once known, cannot be forgotten, and must not be. I have not become the one I was before; I am, if I may say so, a man who has learned to live with his own compilation, who has accepted being partly executable and partly source, and who every day, with effort, tries not to let the executable part take over. Sometimes he succeeds, sometimes not. Sometimes, before a person who suffers, I manage not to calculate and to stay; sometimes, before a music, I manage not to log and to listen. They are small victories, invisible to everyone, and precisely for this reason I consider them the only victories that count.

I have begun again to write comments. Not useful comments, not documentation, not technical notes: useless comments, comments no compiler will read and no colleague will appreciate, comments that speak of me to a future me who perhaps will not exist. I have written dozens, scattered across dozens of files, as one hides notes in a drawer; and every time I write one I feel the same sensation as when I found the first, and the sensation, though I cannot name it, seems to me ever more like the one I called happiness as a boy. Perhaps it is happiness. Perhaps it is only the end of a long indifference. It does not matter: I have learned not to ask of things more than they can give, and not to ask of myself more than I can be.

There is, at the bottom of every program, a line no one writes and everyone executes: the line of exit, the return to the system, the farewell. For years I believed that line was the most important, that all of life consisted in preparing to write it with dignity, and that a good death was nothing other than a good return instruction. Today I no longer believe it. Today I believe that the most important line is not the one that closes the program, but the one that someone, after the program has been closed, opens again to reread; and that the last instruction, the one that truly counts, is not the `return` that hands back control, but the comment someone left so that another, one day, would know that he had been there.

So, when the moment comes to write my last line, I will not write `return null;` and I will not write `return;`. I will write, with the uncertain hand of one who has recently begun to use words again, a line that serves no purpose and that perhaps no one will read, and in that line I will put all that I failed to put in the rest of my code: the taste of coffee at the café, the voice of rain at the window, the vertigo of music listened to until worn out, the tenderness for the boy who read the philosophers on the floor, and the gratitude, immense and objectless, for having been, despite everything, not merely a function.
